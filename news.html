Basics

How a Home Solar + Battery System Works

A home solar + storage system turns sunlight into electricity you can use now and save for later. Understanding its four building blocks helps you choose the right parts and avoid costly mismatches.

Solar panels capture sunlight and produce direct current (DC) electricity. A charge controller — usually built into the inverter — regulates that flow so the battery charges safely.

The battery stores energy as DC for use when the sun is down or the grid fails. The inverter then converts DC into the alternating current (AC) your appliances use (230V/50Hz in most of Africa and India, 120V/60Hz in some regions).

A typical day: panels power your home first, any surplus charges the battery, and at night or during an outage the battery discharges back through the inverter. Size each part to match the others and you avoid wasted money or unexpected blackouts.

Batteries

What Is LiFePO4 and Why It Lasts Longer

For years lead-acid batteries dominated off-grid storage. Today lithium iron phosphate — LiFePO4, often written LFP — has become the standard for home systems, and for good reasons. It costs more upfront, but its lower cost per cycle over a long life usually makes it the cheaper choice in the end.

  • Cycle life: 2,000–6,000+ cycles vs roughly 500–1,000 for lead-acid
  • Safer chemistry: very stable, far less prone to overheating or thermal runaway
  • More usable capacity in less space and weight per kWh
  • Low self-discharge and essentially maintenance-free
  • Handles partial charging well, so you are not forced into full cycles
Inverters

Off-Grid vs Grid-Tied vs Hybrid Inverters

An inverter is the brain of the system, but not all inverters work the same way. The three common types suit very different situations.

Grid-tied: connects to the utility grid and feeds surplus power back. It is the cheapest option and great where the grid is stable — but it shuts down automatically during a grid outage, so it provides no backup.

Off-grid: runs with no grid connection at all. The battery is central, and the system is sized for worst-case days. Best for remote homes and villages without reliable power.

Hybrid: combines both. It can prioritize solar → battery → grid, use the grid as a backup source, and often accept a generator input. For areas with frequent outages — common across parts of Africa and India — hybrid is usually the most practical choice.

Tip: 'hybrid' features vary a lot between products. Check whether it supports stacking (parallel units), generator start, and the backup switchover time before you buy.

Sizing

How to Size a Home Battery (Step by Step)

Sizing a battery is not guesswork. Follow five steps using your own real loads.

Step 1 — List essentials: fridge, lights, fans, router, TV, and anything you must keep running. Note each item's wattage (usually on a label).

Step 2 — Estimate daily energy: for each item, watts × hours used per day = watt-hours (Wh). Add them up to get your daily kWh.

Step 3 — Decide backup time: one day, two days, or more? Multiply your daily kWh by the number of days you want to cover.

Step 4 — Apply depth of discharge: LiFePO4 is safely usable to about 80%, so divide by 0.8. Battery capacity (Wh) ≈ daily kWh × backup days ÷ 0.8.

Step 5 — Match the inverter: its continuous wattage should exceed your peak load (everything on at once) by 20–30%. Size for essentials, not the whole house, to keep the system affordable.

Hot Climate

Protecting Batteries in Hot Climates (Africa & India)

Heat is the quiet enemy of battery life. Roughly speaking, every 10°C above 25°C can halve a battery's expected cycle life. In hot regions this matters as much as the battery you buy.

Install in shade: never place batteries in direct sun or inside a sealed metal box. A ventilated, shaded enclosure is ideal.

Keep it cool: aim for 20–35°C. Allow airflow around the unit and avoid stacking it against inverters or charge controllers, which give off their own heat.

A small ventilation fan or louvered door helps in enclosed spaces. If you can monitor temperature, do — early heat is easier to fix than a failed bank.

For long idle periods, store at 50–80% charge rather than a full 100%. This is kinder to the cells than sitting fully charged for months.

Buying

How to Read a Warranty — Don't Trust '10 Years'

A '10-year warranty' printed on a box tells you almost nothing by itself. What matters is what the warranty actually guarantees.

Throughput: look for total energy the battery is guaranteed to deliver — often stated as a cycle count (e.g., 6,000 cycles) or a lifetime kWh figure. This is far more meaningful than years alone.

Capacity retention: a good warranty promises a minimum remaining capacity at the end of the term, commonly ≥80%.

Exclusions: read what is NOT covered — shipping, labor, misuse, flood, or grid events. A vague '10 years' that excludes everything is worthless.

Who honors it: confirm whether the manufacturer or the seller stands behind it, and for how long in your local market. A 10-year / 6,000-cycle / 80%-retention warranty from a responsive supplier beats a vague claim from an unknown brand.
